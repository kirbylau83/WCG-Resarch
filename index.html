Clinical Trials: Hope Through Research

Participating in a clinical trial may provide access to innovative treatments

What is a Clinical Trial?

A clinical trial is a research study that tests new ways to prevent, detect, treat, or manage various health conditions and diseases. These studies are essential for developing better treatments and improving patient care.

For liver disease patients, clinical trials offer the opportunity to access cutting-edge therapies that are not yet widely available, while contributing to medical knowledge that may help future patients.

Why Participate in a Clinical Trial?

✓ Access to New Treatments

Receive innovative therapies before they become publicly available

✓ Expert Medical Care

Close monitoring by specialized healthcare professionals

✓ Contribute to Science

Help advance medical knowledge for future patients

✓ No Cost Treatment

Study medication and most assessments provided at no charge

Is a Clinical Trial Right for You?

Participation in a clinical trial is completely voluntary and is a personal decision. We encourage you to:

  • Take your time to learn about the study and ask questions
  • Discuss with your family, friends, and healthcare providers
  • Understand the potential risks and benefits
  • Review the time commitment and visit schedule
  • Ask about compensation for time and travel expenses
  • Know that you can withdraw at any time

Our Research Focus: MASH

The WCG Liver Research Centre is currently focused on clinical trials for Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH), previously known as NASH. MASH is a serious liver condition that can lead to cirrhosis and liver failure if left untreated.

We are actively enrolling patients in multiple Phase 2 and Phase 3 clinical trials investigating promising new treatments for MASH.

Frequently Asked Questions

Understanding MASH clinical trial assessments

What is a liver biopsy and why is it required?

A liver biopsy is a medical procedure where a small sample of liver tissue is removed for examination under a microscope. In MASH trials, biopsies are essential to confirm the diagnosis, assess the degree of inflammation, fat accumulation, and fibrosis (scarring), and evaluate treatment effectiveness. The procedure is typically performed using a thin needle guided by ultrasound imaging. While generally safe, it carries minor risks including discomfort, bleeding, or infection. Most trial participants have biopsies at baseline and at study completion to measure changes.

What is MRI-PDFF (Proton Density Fat Fraction)?

MRI-PDFF is a non-invasive imaging technique that precisely measures the amount of fat in your liver. Unlike traditional ultrasound, MRI-PDFF provides quantitative data, showing the exact percentage of liver fat. This test is painless and involves lying still in an MRI scanner for approximately 15-30 minutes. Many MASH trials use MRI-PDFF as a secondary endpoint to track fat reduction over time without requiring repeated biopsies.

What is FibroScan (Transient Elastography)?

FibroScan is a non-invasive ultrasound-based test that measures liver stiffness, which correlates with the degree of fibrosis (scarring). The device sends a painless vibration through the liver while measuring how quickly it travels through the tissue. Stiffer livers indicate more advanced fibrosis. The test takes only 5-10 minutes and requires no special preparation beyond fasting for a few hours. It's commonly used in clinical trials to monitor disease progression or improvement without the need for biopsy.

What blood tests are typically performed?

MASH clinical trials routinely monitor several blood markers including liver enzymes (ALT, AST), lipid panels (cholesterol, triglycerides), glucose and HbA1c for diabetes screening, complete blood counts, and kidney function tests. Specialized markers like cytokeratin-18 fragments and fibrosis scores (FIB-4, ELF) may also be measured. These tests help assess liver inflammation, metabolic health, and treatment safety. Blood draws are typically performed every 4-12 weeks during the trial.

What is the NAS (NAFLD Activity Score)?

The NAS is a scoring system used by pathologists to evaluate liver biopsy samples. It combines three features: steatosis (fat accumulation, scored 0-3), lobular inflammation (scored 0-3), and hepatocellular ballooning (scored 0-2). The total score ranges from 0-8. A score of 5 or higher typically indicates MASH, while 3-4 is borderline. Most clinical trials require a minimum NAS score for enrollment and use improvements in NAS as a primary endpoint to demonstrate treatment effectiveness.

How often are study visits required?

Study visit frequency varies by trial phase and design. Early phase trials may require weekly or biweekly visits initially, while Phase 3 trials typically involve monthly visits during dose escalation, then every 1-3 months during the maintenance phase. Visits usually include vital signs, physical examination, blood work, and adverse event assessment. Some trials also include telehealth check-ins between in-person visits. The total study duration can range from 24 weeks for Phase 2 trials to 240 weeks (approximately 5 years) for long-term Phase 3 studies.

Are there dietary or lifestyle requirements?

Most MASH trials encourage healthy lifestyle modifications including balanced diet and regular exercise, as these improve outcomes regardless of treatment. However, dramatic weight loss or starting new weight-loss medications during the trial may be prohibited, as this could interfere with assessing the study drug's effectiveness. Alcohol consumption must typically be minimal or zero. You'll receive detailed guidance from the study team about permitted foods, supplements, and activities.

What are placebo-controlled trials?

Many MASH trials are placebo-controlled, meaning some participants receive the active study medication while others receive an inactive placebo (often a saline injection or sugar pill). This design is considered the gold standard for determining whether a treatment truly works. Participants are randomly assigned to groups and neither they nor their doctors know which treatment they're receiving until the study ends (called "blinding"). All participants receive the same standard-of-care counseling on diet and lifestyle, and are monitored with equal care and attention.
